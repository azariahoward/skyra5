The college route

Majors careers

A major is the subject you specialize in — usually about a third of your classes, with the rest being general education and electives. Three things most people don't get told: you normally don't have to declare until the end of sophomore year, changing your major is common and usually fine, and most jobs don't require one specific major. Tap any major below to see what you'd actually study, the classes involved, and where it leads.

You don't always need a 4-year degree

Plenty of stable, well-paid careers are reached another way. These aren't shortcuts around hard work — they're different roads, often faster and far cheaper, and several out-earn the average bachelor's degree. Here's how each actually works.

How to think about the choice

The real question isn't "college or not" — it's what does this specific career require, and what will this specific path cost me? A nurse and an electrician both need training; they just need different training. Three things worth weighing:

  • Total cost, not sticker price. Compare tuition plus the years you're not earning. A 2-year path that pays you while you learn can be tens of thousands ahead.
  • Is the credential required by law? You cannot be a nurse, electrician, or lawyer without the license. For many other jobs, skill and portfolio matter more than the diploma.
  • Doors it keeps open. Degrees are portable across fields; trade licenses are deep but specific. Both can lead to owning a business.

You can also mix them: community college first then transfer, a trade while taking night classes, or military service that pays for college afterward.

Another way to read the job world

The four job collars

Long before anyone said "career path," people sorted jobs by the color of the shirt collar workers wore. The labels stuck. They're a quick way to picture the kind of environment, training, and pay a job tends to involve — though plenty of real jobs blend more than one.

Heads up: these are broad, old-fashioned categories with fuzzy edges. A nurse (pink) is also highly skilled; a developer (white) might be self-taught like a trade. Use them as a lens, not a label that boxes you in.

Skip the four-year price tag

College & certificate paths that pay

A four-year degree is one road — not the only one. Community-college associate degrees, short certificates, and paid apprenticeships lead to real, well-paid careers in months to two years, often with little or no debt. Here are some of the strongest, and how to start.

How to start

1. Find your local community college. Search "[your area] community college programs" — they list every certificate and associate degree and what each leads to.

2. File the FAFSA (studentaid.gov). Community college is already low-cost, and aid or scholarships often cover much of it.

3. Look for "stackable" credentials. Many certificates count toward an associate degree later, so you can start earning fast and keep building.

4. Ask about transfer agreements. If you might want a four-year degree eventually, many community colleges guarantee credit transfer to state universities — saving thousands.

Pay figures are typical U.S. medians and vary by location, employer, and experience.

Two rules that matter more than any single step

Keep your options open early, then narrow. In middle school and early high school, breadth beats specialization — try things, and build the habits and grades that quietly decide what's available later. By 11th and 12th, go deep on the direction that fits.

Depth beats a long list. One club you led or one project you finished says more than ten things you joined and quit. Colleges and employers both read commitment, not clutter.
