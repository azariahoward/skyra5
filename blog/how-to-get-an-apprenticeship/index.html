How to Get an Apprenticeship (and What They Actually Pay)

Updated October 2, 2026 · ~6 min read · By Skyra Roadmap

An apprenticeship is one of the few paths where you get paid to learn a career — no tuition, no debt, and a job at the end. Here's how they work and how to land one.

Think of an apprenticeship as "earn while you learn." You work alongside skilled pros, collect a paycheck from day one, get raises as you improve, and finish with a recognized credential — often a journeyman license that sets you up for strong pay or your own business.

How apprenticeships work

  • Paid on-the-job training — the bulk of your time, learning by doing under a mentor.
  • Some classroom instruction — usually a few hours a week, often free or employer-paid.
  • Scheduled raises — you start lower and your wage steps up as your skills grow.
  • A credential at the end — a nationally recognized certification and, in many trades, a path to licensure.

What they actually pay

Apprentices typically start around 40–50% of a fully-trained worker's wage and climb from there with each milestone. The headline isn't the starting number — it's that you're earning the whole time instead of paying tuition. Compare that to borrowing for four years, and the math often favors the apprentice for years. See what the finished trades pay in highest-paying trade jobs.

Where to find one

  1. apprenticeship.gov — the U.S. Department of Labor's official finder, searchable by trade and location.
  2. Your state apprenticeship agency — most states run their own registered programs.
  3. Local trade unions — many of the best-paid apprenticeships (electrical, elevator, plumbing) run through union locals.
  4. Community colleges — often partner with employers to place apprentices.
  5. Employers directly — search "[your state] + [trade] + apprenticeship," or just ask local shops if they take apprentices.

How to apply and stand out

  • Meet the basics: most programs want you 18+ (some 16–17 with limits), with a high school diploma or GED and a driver's license.
  • Lead with reliability and attitude. Employers invest years in apprentices — they hire for dependability and willingness to learn more than existing skill.
  • Show up in person. Visiting a shop or union hall and asking face-to-face makes a stronger impression than an online form.
  • Get a little relevant exposure first — a shop-class project, a helper job, or a short pre-apprenticeship program signals you're serious.
Good to know: registered apprenticeships are competitive for a reason — they're a genuinely great deal. If you don't get in the first round, a pre-apprenticeship or helper role is a common stepping stone. Persistence is part of the application.

Frequently asked questions

How do apprenticeships work?

An apprenticeship combines paid on-the-job training with some classroom instruction. You work alongside skilled professionals, earn a paycheck from day one, and get raises as your skills grow — finishing with a recognized credential and often a journeyman license. Most run 1–5 years depending on the trade.

How much do apprentices get paid?

Apprentices typically start around 40–50% of a fully-trained worker's wage and get scheduled raises as they progress — so pay rises steadily over the program. The exact number varies by trade and region, but the key point is you earn (not pay) the entire time, and graduate with no tuition debt.

How do I find an apprenticeship?

Start with the U.S. Department of Labor's apprenticeship finder (apprenticeship.gov), your state's apprenticeship agency, local trade unions, community colleges, and employers directly. Searching '[your state] + [trade] + apprenticeship' turns up most programs.

Are apprenticeships better than college?

For skilled-trade careers, often yes — you avoid debt, earn immediately, and finish with a credential and a job. For careers that require a degree, college is still the path. It comes down to the specific career you want, not a blanket rule.
