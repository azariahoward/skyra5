How to Write a Resume With No Work Experience (Teen Guide)

Updated October 2, 2026 · ~5 min read · By Skyra Roadmap

Everyone's first resume has the same "problem": no jobs yet. Here's the secret — you have more to put on it than you think, and employers hiring teens know you're just starting.

A first resume isn't about proving you've worked. It's about showing you're reliable, capable, and worth training. You do that with what you've already done — in school, in your community, and on your own. Here's how to build it.

What to include instead of jobs

  • A one-line objective — e.g., "Dependable high school student seeking a part-time role in customer service, known for reliability and a strong work ethic."
  • Education — your school, expected graduation year, and GPA if it's strong.
  • Skills — real ones: communication, teamwork, basic computer skills, a second language, time management.
  • Volunteer work — any community service shows initiative and responsibility.
  • Extracurriculars — sports (teamwork), clubs (commitment), leadership roles.
  • Informal work — babysitting, lawn care, pet sitting, tutoring all count as real work. List them.
  • Awards & projects — honor roll, a science-fair project, a personal project you built or ran.

A simple template

Your Name
City · Phone · Professional email

Objective — One line on what you're looking for and what you bring.

Education — School name, expected graduation, GPA (if strong).

Skills — 4–6 relevant skills.

Experience & Activities — Volunteering, informal work, clubs, sports — with a short bullet on what you did.

Awards — Any recognition or achievements.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Going over one page. Keep it tight and scannable.
  • An unprofessional email. Make one that's just your name — skip the childhood nickname.
  • Typos. Proofread, then have an adult read it. Errors read as careless.
  • Being vague. "Helped out" is weak; "Babysat 3 kids weekly, managed meals and bedtime" shows real responsibility.
  • Lying or padding. Don't. Honest and modest beats impressive and false, every time.
Pro tip: match your resume to the job. Applying to a café? Lead with people skills and reliability. A tutoring gig? Lead with your strong subjects and any teaching or mentoring. Small tweaks make a big difference.

Frequently asked questions

How do you write a resume with no work experience?

Lead with what you do have: school achievements, volunteer work, clubs, sports, projects, and skills. Use a short 'objective' line, highlight transferable skills like reliability and teamwork, and include any informal work (babysitting, lawn care, tutoring). One clean page is plenty.

What should a teen put on a resume?

Contact info, a one-line objective, education (your school and expected graduation), relevant skills, and sections for volunteering, extracurriculars, awards, and any informal jobs. Anything that shows responsibility, reliability, or initiative belongs on it.

How long should a teen's resume be?

One page — always. At this stage a single, clean, well-organized page is far more effective than padding. Employers hiring teens spend seconds scanning; make it easy.

Do you need a resume for your first job?

Not always — many first jobs just need an application — but having one makes you look serious and prepared, and it helps you organize what you'd say in an interview. For babysitting or neighborhood gigs it's optional; for retail, food service, or anything formal, it helps you stand out.
