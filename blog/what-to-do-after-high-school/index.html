What to Do After High School: 7 Paths Besides a 4-Year College

Updated October 2, 2026 · ~7 min read · By Skyra Roadmap

College isn't the only road to a good life — and for a lot of teens, it isn't even the best one. Here are seven real paths after high school, who each one fits, and how to pick.

The pressure to "just go to college" leaves a lot of graduates with debt and no direction. The truth is there have never been more good options. Here's the honest rundown of what's actually available.

The 7 paths

Earn while you learn

1. Skilled-trade apprenticeship

Get paid from day one while training as an electrician, plumber, HVAC tech, or welder. No tuition debt, strong pay, and jobs that can't be shipped overseas. Best for hands-on people who want to start earning fast.

Cheap, flexible, and underrated

2. Community college

Two-year programs lead to licensed careers (nursing, dental hygiene, IT) for a fraction of university cost — or transfer to a four-year school after two years and save thousands on the same degree. Best for keeping options open on a budget.

Still right for some careers

3. Four-year college

If your teen wants a career that requires a degree — nursing, engineering, teaching, law — college is the path. The key is choosing it on purpose, with a career in mind, not as a default. Best when the career clearly needs it.

Training, pay, and tuition benefits

4. The military

Steady income, job training in hundreds of fields, and education benefits (GI Bill) that can pay for college later. A serious commitment, but a real launchpad. Best for those who want structure and skills with their future paid for.

Start earning, stack skills

5. Work + certifications

Go straight into a job and add short certifications (IT, coding, real estate, CDL) that boost pay over time. Best for teens who are done with classrooms and learn better by doing.

Explore, then decide

6. Gap year (with a plan)

A structured year of work, travel, volunteering, or interning — not a year on the couch. Done right, it helps a burned-out or undecided teen return to school or work with direction. Best when there's an actual plan.

Entrepreneurship & freelancing

7. Start something

Freelance a skill, sell online, or build a small business. Risky and not for everyone, but the cheapest time to try is before big obligations pile up. Best for self-starters with an idea and grit.

How to choose between them

Don't start with the path — start with the person and the career, then work backward to the path that gets there cheapest and fastest. Three questions cut through most of the noise:

  • Does the career they want actually require a degree? If yes, college or the "2+2" community-college route. If no, a trade or certification may get them there sooner and debt-free. (See Is College Worth It?)
  • How do they learn best? Classroom learners and hands-on learners thrive on very different paths. Forcing a hands-on teen through four more years of lectures rarely ends well.
  • How soon do they need (or want) to earn? Apprenticeships and work pay now; degrees pay later. Both are valid — it's about the trade-off that fits your family.
If your teen says "I don't know," that's normal — most don't. Read our playbook for the "I have no idea" phase, then come back to this list with a direction or two in mind.

The one mistake to avoid

The costly mistake isn't picking the "wrong" path — it's picking by default, without looking at the alternatives. A teen who chooses a trade on purpose will almost always do better than one who drifts into a four-year degree because it's what everyone else did. Choose on purpose, whatever you choose.

Frequently asked questions

What can you do after high school besides college?

Plenty: a paid skilled-trade apprenticeship, community college, the military, going straight to work while stacking certifications, a structured gap year, or starting a business. College is one good path — not the only one.

What is the best option after high school?

There's no single best option — the right one depends on the career your teen wants and how they learn. Someone aiming at nursing should look at college or community college; someone who loves working with their hands may do far better (and earn sooner) in a trade apprenticeship. Start by figuring out the fit.

What should I do after high school if I have no idea?

Start small and low-pressure. Take a career-fit quiz to surface a few directions, then explore two of them through a conversation or a short trial. You don't need a 10-year plan — you need the next step.
