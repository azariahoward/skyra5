Best First Jobs for Teens (14–17) and How to Get One

Updated October 2, 2026 · ~6 min read · By Skyra Roadmap

A first job does more than earn money — it teaches you how to show up, work with people, and handle responsibility. Here are the best first jobs for teens, plus how to actually land one.

The real value of a first job isn't the paycheck (nice as it is) — it's the skills employers and colleges can't teach in a classroom: reliability, communication, and grit. Here's where to start.

10 solid first jobs for teens

Typical age: 15–16+

1. Grocery / retail associate

Stocking, cashiering, and customer service. Reliable hours, teaches money-handling and showing up on time. The classic first job for a reason.

Typical age: 14–16+

2. Food service / fast food

Fast pace, teamwork, and real customer skills. Tips in some roles; flexible around school schedules.

Typical age: 15–16+ (certified)

3. Lifeguard

Higher pay than most teen jobs, plus responsibility and a certification that looks great later. Seasonal and social.

Typical age: 15–16+

4. Camp counselor

Leadership, patience, and working with kids. Great for teens eyeing teaching, coaching, or health careers.

Typical age: 14+

5. Babysitter / pet sitter

Flexible, self-directed, and you set your rate. A gateway to running a tiny business — market yourself to neighbors.

Typical age: 15+

6. Tutor

Teach a subject you're strong in. Pays well per hour and builds communication skills colleges and employers love.

Typical age: 14–15+

7. Lawn care / landscaping

Outdoor, physical, and easy to turn into your own weekend business. Good intro to the trades mindset.

Typical age: 16+

8. Barista / café help

People skills, speed, and handling money. Popular, social, and flexible.

Typical age: 15–16+

9. Movie theater / amusement venue

Fun environment, lots of teen coworkers, customer-service reps. Often flexible evening and weekend hours.

Typical age: 14+ (with a parent)

10. Freelance online gigs

Simple graphic design, video editing, or social-media help. Builds a portfolio and digital skills early — supervise younger teens.

Know the rules before you apply

Teen work is protected by law, and the rules depend on your age and state:

  • Ages 14–15: limited hours (not during school, capped per day/week) and certain jobs are off-limits for safety. Lots is still open — especially self-directed gigs like babysitting and lawn care.
  • Ages 16–17: more hours and more job types open up, though some hazardous work is still restricted until 18.
  • Work permits: many states require one for workers under 16 (sometimes under 18), usually through your school. Check your state labor department first.

How to land one with zero experience

  1. Lead with reliability. You can't show experience you don't have — so sell the thing employers want most from teens: dependability. "I show up on time and I learn fast" beats a padded résumé.
  2. Line up one reference. A teacher, coach, or neighbor who'll vouch for you matters more than a long work history.
  3. Apply in person where you can. Walking in, neatly dressed, and asking for the manager stands out in a pile of online applications.
  4. Keep the application clean. No typos, a real email address, and honest availability. Need a résumé? A one-pager is plenty at this stage.
Parent tip: Let your teen own the process — writing the message, making the call, walking in. The confidence from landing it themselves is half the point.

Pick a first job that points somewhere

If your teen has an inkling of a career direction, a first job can be a cheap test-drive: a kid curious about health care can lifeguard or volunteer at a clinic; a future entrepreneur can run a lawn or pet business. Frequently asked questions

What is the best first job for a teenager?

There's no single best one — the best first job is reliable, fits around school, and teaches transferable skills like showing up on time, handling money, and dealing with customers. Retail, food service, lifeguarding, and tutoring are all strong starters. Pick based on your teen's personality as much as the pay.

What jobs can a 14 or 15 year old get?

At 14–15, U.S. federal law limits hours and job types, but plenty is open: babysitting, pet sitting, lawn care, tutoring, camp help, and some retail or food roles. Many of these don't even require a formal employer — a teen can offer the service directly to neighbors.

How can a teen get a job with no experience?

Everyone starts with no experience. Lead with reliability and attitude, not a résumé: a short, clean application, a neat appearance, and the ability to say 'I'm dependable and I learn fast.' A reference from a teacher, coach, or neighbor goes a long way. Apply in person where you can — it stands out.

Do teens need a work permit?

It depends on your state. Many states require working teens under 16 (sometimes under 18) to get a work permit or 'age certificate,' often issued through their school. Check your state's labor department before the first day.
